The experiment in one paragraph

The group (Paul Wrede, Nima Mahkam, Daniel Razansky) can already trap and steer micro-particles in flowing blood with focused ultrasound (FUS), and watch them in 3D with optoacoustic (OA) tomography. That is Kim et al. 2024, and Paul Wrede is joint first author. Separately, Purohit et al. 2025 showed that liposomes release drug on demand under low-intensity pulsed ultrasound if their core is made acoustically different from the surroundings (5% sucrose). Your project glues the two together: make sound-responsive liposomes, conjugate them onto microrobots, trap and steer the robots with one ultrasound setting, then release the drug with another setting. You then measure how much was released, where, and whether trapping alone already leaks it.

Make liposomes→ Characterise (DLS, ζ, EE)→ Conjugate to robot→ US trap + steer in flow→ OA image→ US trigger release→ Quantify

The 10 concepts you must have

  1. Acoustic radiation force $F_R$. Sound scattered by a particle pushes it toward pressure nodes or antinodes. It scales with volume ($\propto a^3$) and with the acoustic contrast factor $\Phi$ (density and compressibility mismatch).
  2. Acoustic streaming. Sound loses energy to viscosity, which drives a steady flow. The resulting drag $F_{AD}\propto a$ dominates for small particles ($ka \ll 1$).
  3. Stokes drag $F_D = 6\pi\eta a u$. The flow pushes the trap away, and the trap holds only while $F_T \ge F_D$.
  4. Why the "flower" works. Sharp nanosheet tips create local secondary streaming vortices, which make a low Gor'kov potential that pulls neighbours in. A big cluster then gets $F_R \propto a^3$ but drag only $\propto a$, so it holds.
  5. Focused ultrasound parameters: frequency $f$, peak negative pressure (PNP), duty cycle, PRF, and the mechanical index $\mathrm{MI}=\mathrm{PNP}/\sqrt{f}$ (FDA diagnostic limit 1.9).
  6. Liposome basics: a lipid bilayer vesicle (~160 nm), made of HSPC + cholesterol + DSPE-PEG. Made by ethanol injection or thin film, then extrusion. Characterised by size, PDI, zeta potential and encapsulation efficiency.
  7. Remote (active) loading with an ammonium-sulfate gradient. A weak-base drug crosses the bilayer uncharged, gets protonated inside and is trapped.
  8. Acoustic impedance $Z=\rho c$ and the sucrose trick. A denser, stiffer core plus an osmotic gradient makes the liposome respond to ultrasound without gas, heat or cavitation.
  9. Optoacoustics: a ns laser pulse is absorbed, heats, expands and emits ultrasound. $p_0=\Gamma\mu_a F$. Multispectral unmixing (HbO, HbR, ICG) in the NIR window.
  10. Nano–bio interface: protein corona, opsonisation, PEG "stealth", macrophage clearance (liver, spleen), and endocytosis routes vs. size.

Paper 1 · Kim, Wrede, … Razansky, Sitti (2024)

Hierarchical Nanostructures as Acoustically Manipulatable Multifunctional Agents in Dynamic Fluid Flow. Adv. Mater. 36, 2404514. Read from rendered pages 1–14.

Intuition first

Trapping microbubbles with ultrasound is easy because a gas core is very compressible, so it responds strongly. But bubbles are fragile: they burst above MI ≈ 1, and they are hard to modify. Solid microspheres are robust, but in moving fluid a single-beam trap loses them. Kim et al. grow flower-like microparticles made of 2D nanosheets (HNS-MPs, 1–5 µm) and find that these do stay trapped in flow up to ~60 mm/s, which is venous or arteriole speed. The reason is the sharp nanosheet tips. They create local "secondary" streaming vortices, which pull neighbouring particles together into a large cluster at the focus. A big cluster feels a much larger radiation force than one particle, so it beats the flow.

What they did, and why

  1. Made 5 materials (BiOI, ZnO, TiO₂, polyimide PI, Ni-MOF) by hydrothermal self-assembly (Fig 1b–g). This shows the effect comes from the shape, not the chemistry.
  2. Trapped them in tubes (500 µm, 3.18 mm, 4.77 mm ID) with a single 2 MHz FUS transducer at 0.42 MPa, 85 mm away, filmed with a high-speed camera (Fig 2). Solid SiO₂/PS spheres and SonoVue bubbles were the controls.
  3. Simulated it in COMSOL (thermoviscous acoustics plus creeping flow) to find the mechanism (Fig 3).
  4. Made them imaging agents: Au NPs for OCT, ICG@PDA for optoacoustics, Gd doping for MRI. They also measured BET surface area and cytotoxicity (Fig 4).
  5. In vivo: steered 3 simultaneous traps in a mouse femoral vein using the POUS system, which does FUS trapping and 3D OA imaging with the same 512-element array (Fig 5).
  6. As microrobots: photocatalytic dye cleanup and doxorubicin loading in nanoporous carbon, up to 95.6% (Fig 5e–g).

Key equations (Experimental section, p. 14)

Trap condition (p. 5):

$$F_T \ \ge\ F_D,\qquad F_T \approx F_R + F_{AD} + \text{inter-particle forces}$$

Stokes drag from streaming and flow (their Eq. 4):

$$F_{AD} = 6\pi\eta a\,(\langle v\rangle - u)$$

$\eta$ is the viscosity (water 1 mPa·s), $a$ the particle radius (~1–2 µm), $\langle v\rangle$ the time-averaged streaming velocity, and $u$ the particle velocity. Example: a 3.3 µm particle at 51 mm/s gives $6\pi\cdot10^{-3}\cdot1.65\,\mu\text{m}\cdot0.051 \approx 1.6$ nN, matching their Fig 3a my estimate.

Radiation force in a (quasi-)standing wave (Eq. 1) and contrast factor (Eq. 2):

$$F_R = 4\pi\,\Phi\, a^3\, k\, E_{ac}\,\sin(2ky),\qquad \Phi=\frac13\left(\frac{5\rho_p/\rho_m-2}{2\rho_p/\rho_m+1}-\frac{\kappa_p}{\kappa_m}\right)$$

Here $k=2\pi/\lambda$ (they write $q$), $E_{ac}$ is the acoustic energy density, $\rho$ the density, $\kappa$ the compressibility, and $p,m$ mean particle and medium. $\Phi>0$ means the particle goes to pressure nodes. With $E_{ac}=p_a^2/(4\rho_m c_m^2)$ not in papers and $p_a=0.42$ MPa you get $E_{ac}\approx 20$ J/m³. For a 1.75 µm polystyrene sphere ($\Phi\approx0.22$) that gives $F_R\approx 2.5$ pN, about 600× smaller than the nN drag my estimate. That is why single solid particles wash away.

Which force dominates (p. 5):

$$\kappa_{\text{Kim}} = \frac{2\pi r}{\lambda}\ \ll 1 \ \Rightarrow\ \text{streaming drag dominates}$$

At 2 MHz in water, $\lambda = c/f = 1480/2\times10^6 = 0.74$ mm, so $2\pi r/\lambda\sim 10^{-2}$. Don't confuse this $\kappa$ with compressibility. The paper reuses the symbol.

Viscous boundary layer (Eq. 3):

$$\delta=\sqrt{2\nu/\omega}$$
Check this. With $\nu=10^{-6}$ m²/s at 2 MHz I get $\delta\approx0.40$ µm. The paper says 0.32 µm, which matches 3 MHz (0.33 µm). The difference doesn't change their conclusion (δ ≪ tube size), but it's a small inconsistency you could ask about.

Gor'kov potential (Eq. 5): particles move to minima of $U$, and $F_R=-\nabla U$. Standard form not in papers:

$$U=\frac{4\pi a^3}{3}\left[\frac{f_1}{2\rho_m c_m^2}\langle p^2\rangle-\frac{3}{4}f_2\,\rho_m\langle v^2\rangle\right],\quad f_1=1-\frac{\kappa_p}{\kappa_m},\ f_2=\frac{2(\rho_p-\rho_m)}{2\rho_p+\rho_m}$$
Printed equation looks garbled. Their Eq. 5 is printed as $\tfrac{4\pi a^3}{3}\big(\tfrac12 f_1|p|^2-\tfrac43 f_2|v|^2\big)$. The $\rho c^2$ and $\rho$ factors are missing and $\tfrac43$ should be $\tfrac34$. Use the standard form above (Bruus/Settnes convention). This was read from the figure of p. 14, not from extracted text.

Numbers to remember

Single-beam FUS at 2 MHz and 0.42 MPa, and POUS at 3 MHz with 512 elements, ~2.8 MPa and MI 1.6. Trapping in flow up to 59.5 mm/s. Mouse femoral vein flow ~16 mm/s. Particle concentration 1–5 mg/mL. OA laser at 20 Hz and 760/800/850 nm. 24 ms alternation between trapping and imaging gives 20 volumes/s.

What could go wrong (and what to ask)

  • The 2D simulation of a 3D flower, with the fluid treated as inviscid in the tube, is qualitative only. The authors say so.
  • Trapping needs high local concentration, and in vivo the particles dilute. This matters for you, because liposome-coated robots will be fewer and heavier.
  • Coating changes the tips. If liposomes (~160 nm) cover the nanosheet edges, they may blunt the streaming hot spots that make trapping work. my inference This is probably the central risk of your project.
  • They state "130 µm-sized FUS focal spots" at 3 MHz, where λ ≈ 0.5 mm. That is below λ/2 (~250 µm), so ask how it's defined (p. 9).
  • Bare BiOI is cytotoxic. Material choice matters for in vivo work.

Paper 2 · Purohit, Yu, … Airan (2025)

Acoustically activatable liposomes as a translational nanotechnology for site-targeted drug delivery and noninvasive neuromodulation. Nat. Nanotech. 20, 1688–1699. Read pp. 1–5 rendered, Methods and Extended Data tables from text.

Intuition first

Older ultrasound-triggered carriers use a gas or perfluorocarbon core (which can cavitate, are unstable, and load drug poorly) or heat (slow and hard to control). Airan's group asked: can I make an ordinary, clinically approved-style liposome sound-sensitive by changing only what's inside it? They add 5% sucrose to the core. That makes the core slightly denser and stiffer than blood plasma (an acoustic contrast) and strongly hyperosmotic. Ultrasound shakes the membrane, the membrane briefly becomes leaky, and the osmotic gradient pulls water in, which pushes drug out. There is no gas, no measurable heating and no cavitation signature.

What they did, and why

  1. Built AALs (acoustically activatable liposomes): HSPC + cholesterol + DSPE-PEG2000, ~160 nm, loaded with ketamine or local anaesthetics (lidocaine, bupivacaine, ropivacaine) by an ammonium-sulfate gradient.
  2. Varied the core: 0%, 5% or 10% sucrose, and equi-osmolar NaCl or glucose. This separates acoustic effects from osmotic effects (Fig 1d,e).
  3. In vitro release: 250 kHz, 25% duty, 5 Hz PRF, 60 s, in plasma. Release was measured by separating free from liposomal drug on a column, then HPLC.
  4. Ruled out the alternatives: no heating (+0.1–0.2 °C), no broadband or harmonic emissions (so no cavitation), no gas in cryo-EM, no blood–brain barrier opening.
  5. In vivo: targeted ketamine to one rat brain region (<5 mm resolution), changed ECoG and behaviour, and produced a sciatic-nerve block with ropivacaine.

$Z$ is the acoustic impedance (MPa·s/m) and $K$ the bulk modulus (compressibility $\kappa=1/K$). Measured values (Extended Data Table 2):

Release scales linearly with core impedance, from ~20% at 0% sucrose to ~60% at 10% ($r^2=0.97$, Fig 1e). The release metric:

$$\%\text{uncaging}=\frac{\text{drug in free fraction}}{\text{free}+\text{liposomal}}\times100\%$$

Mechanical index:

$$\mathrm{MI}=\frac{\mathrm{PNP\,[MPa]}}{\sqrt{f\,[\mathrm{MHz}]}}=\frac{0.9}{\sqrt{0.25}}=1.8$$

This is just under the FDA diagnostic limit of 1.9. Release scaled with MI better than with pressure alone (Ext. Data Fig 1), which points to a mechanical rather than thermal mechanism.

Two Fermi checks my estimate:

  • Intensity reflection at the core/plasma boundary is $R=\left(\frac{Z_2-Z_1}{Z_2+Z_1}\right)^2\approx(0.042/3.16)^2\approx2\times10^{-4}$. That's tiny, so "reflection" alone cannot be the mechanism. Think radiation force or oscillation acting on a weak contrast, across many cycles.
  • Osmotic pressure difference (van 't Hoff): $\Pi=\Delta c\,RT\approx0.355\ \text{osmol/L}\times8.314\times310\approx0.9$ MPa. That's comparable to the ultrasound PNP. A large stored energy waits for the membrane to become leaky.

Recipe (Methods), which you will likely run

  1. Dissolve lipids in hot ethanol, dilute to 10% ethanol in 250 mM ammonium sulfate (± 5% sucrose). This forms multilamellar vesicles.
  2. Extrude 10× through a 200 nm polycarbonate filter at 65–70 °C (above the HSPC transition temperature, ~52 °C not in papers). This makes them unilamellar.
  3. Tangential-flow filtration (TFF) against HEPES/NaCl (+ sucrose) creates the transmembrane ammonium gradient.
  4. Add drug and heat at 55 °C for 1.5 h. Active loading happens here.
  5. Run TFF again to remove free drug, then filter-sterilise (220 nm) and store at 4 °C. They were stable for 90 days.
Likely typo. Methods give "HSPC/DSPE-PEG2000/cholesterol 52.8:42.3:4.8 molar". 42% PEG-lipid is implausible. The usual Doxil-type ratio is HSPC : cholesterol : DSPE-PEG ≈ 56 : 38 : 5, so cholesterol and PEG are probably swapped my inference. Ask what ratio your lab uses.

QC numbers (Ext. Data Table 1): size 158–170 nm, PDI 0.06–0.09, zeta potential −43 to −55 mV, ketamine encapsulation efficiency 24–29% (local anaesthetics 36–44%), free drug <5%.

What could go wrong

  • The mechanism is not proven. The authors write that it "appears" mechanical.
  • Their trigger frequency is 250 kHz. Your trapping runs at 2–3 MHz. Will trapping trigger release, or will release need a second transducer? This is the key design question for your project my inference.
  • The column (SEC) separation and the HPLC baseline (sham ~10% "release") put a floor under what you can measure.
  • Release never reaches 100%, because of ultrasound field heterogeneity and assay limits.

Paper 3 · Chen, Gezginer, … Deán-Ben, Razansky (2024)

Multimodal optoacoustic imaging: methods and contrast materials. Chem. Soc. Rev. 53, 6068–6099. Read pp. 1–5 rendered, headings of the rest.

Intuition first

Light gives great molecular contrast but scatters within ~1 mm of tissue. Ultrasound travels centimetres with little scattering but sees only mechanical contrast. Optoacoustics shines a nanosecond laser pulse in. Wherever light is absorbed (haemoglobin, melanin, ICG, gold), the tissue heats by millikelvins, expands, and emits an ultrasound wave. The transducers "hear" where light was absorbed, so you get optical contrast with ultrasound resolution, several mm to cm deep. Using several wavelengths lets you unmix oxygenated from deoxygenated blood, or a dye from blood. This review catalogues combinations of OA with US, fluorescence, OCT, Raman, MRI, CT, SPECT and PET, and the contrast agents for each.

Key equations

Not in the paper. The review has no equations. These are the standard OA relations you will be expected to know.
$$p_0=\Gamma\,\mu_a\,F,\qquad \Gamma=\frac{\beta c^2}{C_p}$$

$p_0$ is the initial pressure, $\Gamma$ the Grüneisen parameter (~0.1–0.2 for water/tissue), $\mu_a$ the absorption coefficient (blood at ~800 nm is a few cm⁻¹), and $F$ the fluence (skin safety limit at 800 nm ≈ 32 mJ/cm²). Example: $0.2\times4\,\text{cm}^{-1}\times20\,\text{mJ/cm}^2\approx16$ kPa my estimate.

Stress confinement: the pulse must be shorter than the time for sound to cross the absorber, $\tau_p \ll d/c$. For $d=100$ µm, $d/c\approx65$ ns, so a ~10 ns pulse works.

Spectral unmixing:

$$\mu_a(\lambda)=\sum_i \varepsilon_i(\lambda)\,C_i$$

This is solved per voxel for HbO, HbR and ICG from several wavelengths. Kim used 760 vs. 850 nm to tell veins from arteries.

Figures you need

Fig 1a. Principle: laser → absorbers in blood → spherical acoustic waves → probe.
Fig 1b. Absorption spectra (log scale, 400–1400 nm) of HbO, HbR, melanin, water, lipid, collagen and ICG. Blood dominates the visible. In NIR-I (~700–1000 nm) everything is weaker, so light goes deeper. ICG peaks near 800 nm, which is why Kim coated particles with ICG.
Fig 1c. Scale: microscopy (µm resolution, ≤1 mm deep) → mesoscopy → tomography (cm deep). Depth/resolution ≈ 200 along the dashed line.
Fig 1d. Radar chart of OA vs. other modalities. OA is strong in functional contrast and time resolution and weaker in penetration and anatomy, which is the case for combining modalities.
Fig 2 and Table 1. OA–US hybrids: the same array for both modes, speed-of-sound maps, porphyrin microbubbles and phase-change nanodroplets as dual contrast agents.

Why it matters for you

  • POUS (Kim Fig 5) is an OA–US hybrid of exactly this kind. One array traps and images.
  • Your liposome-coated robot needs OA contrast (ICG, PDA, Au) without the laser heating the liposome and leaking drug. Ask about this my inference.
  • Liposomes appear in the review as OA/fluorescence carriers (liposomal ICG), so loading a dye in the bilayer is a known route.

Paper 4 · Behzadi, … Mahmoudi (2017)

Cellular uptake of nanoparticles: journey inside the cell. Chem. Soc. Rev. 46, 4218–4244. Read pp. 1–8 rendered, the rest from text.

Intuition first

As soon as a particle enters blood it gets coated in proteins (the protein corona). Cells "see" the corona, not your designed surface. Some of those proteins (opsonins) are "eat me" flags for macrophages, which is why most injected particles end up in the liver and spleen. PEG blocks protein adsorption, which raises circulation half-life from minutes to hours (Doxil is the classic example). If a particle reaches a cell, how it enters depends on size, shape, charge and surface chemistry. Most routes end in the lysosome (acidic, digestive), so cargo that must work inside the cell needs to escape.

Entry routes (Sec. 3, Figs 1–6)

Property effects (Sec. 4)

  • Size: ~50 nm is often optimal for receptor-mediated uptake (Au NPs in HeLa). There is a minimum wrapping radius from adhesion energy vs. membrane bending.
  • Charge: cationic particles are taken up more but are more toxic. Anionic or PEGylated ones circulate longer. The AALs are ζ ≈ −45 mV.
  • Shape and hydrophobicity: rods vs. spheres differ. Hydrophobic surfaces attract opsonins.
  • Corona: always measure charge after incubation in serum, not only in buffer.

Why it matters for you

  • A free small-molecule drug released near the target crosses membranes by itself. The liposome is a carrier, not something that needs to enter cells.
  • The 1–5 µm robots will be cleared by macrophages, so ask about circulation time and whether they PEGylate the robot.
  • Kim's robot cytotoxicity data (Fig 4g) plus this review give you the vocabulary for biocompatibility discussions.
Common knowledge, not in this paper. Lysosomal pH ≈ 4.5–5, and endosomal escape strategies (proton sponge, fusogenic lipids) are standard background.

Understanding checks

Kim

K1. Why do solid 3 µm PS spheres trap in still water but wash away at 8.5 mm/s?
The single-particle radiation force is ~pN (∝a³), while Stokes drag at 8.5 mm/s is ~0.26 nN (∝a). Without clustering, drag wins. Smooth spheres also make no tip streaming, so they don't aggregate.
K2. Fermi: what cluster radius makes F_R (∝a³) beat drag (∝a), if a single 1.75 µm particle has F_R ≈ 2.5 pN against 1.6 nN of drag?
The ratio scales as a², so a ≈ 1.75 µm × √(1.6 nN / 2.5 pN) ≈ 1.75 × 25 ≈ 45 µm. This is crude because a cluster is not a solid sphere, but it shows why clusters of tens of µm and more hold. The observed traps are ~mm.
K3. Wavelength at 2 MHz in water? Why are the traps in the big tube ~400 µm apart?
λ = 1480/2×10⁶ ≈ 0.74 mm. A spacing of ~λ/2 ≈ 0.37 mm is consistent with standing-wave pressure nodes from reflections at the tube wall (my inference, not stated in the paper).
K4. What does the "κ = 2πr/λ ≪ 1" criterion tell you, and what's the value here?
For particles much smaller than λ, streaming drag dominates over radiation force, so they follow streaming lines. Here 2π×1.75 µm/740 µm ≈ 0.015.
K5. Name two things that might stop a liposome-coated flower from trapping.
(1) Liposomes cover the sharp tips, which weakens the secondary streaming. (2) Lower effective concentration or a changed density/compressibility contrast. Also possible: liposomes are released or sheared off by the trapping field itself.

Purohit

P1. Why add sucrose and not just NaCl to the core?
At equal osmolarity, sucrose raises ρ and c (impedance) more, and release tracked impedance (r² = 0.97). So the acoustic contrast matters, not only osmolarity.
P2. Compute MI for 0.9 MPa at 250 kHz, and for 2.8 MPa at 3 MHz.
0.9/√0.25 = 1.8 and 2.8/√3 ≈ 1.6. Both are below 1.9.
P3. Explain ammonium-sulfate remote loading in two sentences.
NH₄⁺ inside deprotonates and NH₃ leaks out, leaving the core acidic. A weak-base drug crosses the bilayer neutral, gets protonated inside, can't get back out, and may precipitate with sulfate.
P4. Why did they choose 5% and not 10% sucrose?
10% released more under ultrasound but also leaked in 37 °C plasma without ultrasound (Fig 1c), and in vivo it was no better.
P5. Which three observations argue against heating and cavitation?
The bio-heat estimate is +0.1–0.2 °C and it works at room temperature. There are no broadband or ultraharmonic emissions, unlike with microbubbles. There is no gas in cryo-EM and no BBB opening (Evans Blue).

Optoacoustics and Fermi

F1. Estimate p₀ for blood at 800 nm (μa ≈ 4 cm⁻¹, F = 20 mJ/cm², Γ = 0.2).
p₀ = Γ μa F = 0.2 × 400 m⁻¹ × 200 J/m² ≈ 16 kPa.
F2. Why use ~10 ns pulses rather than µs?
Stress confinement: the heat must be deposited before sound leaves the absorber. d/c for 100 µm is 65 ns, so µs pulses would smear the signal.
F3. Reynolds number in the 500 µm tube at 60 mm/s?
Re = ρuD/η = 1000 × 0.06 × 5×10⁻⁴ / 10⁻³ ≈ 30, so the flow is laminar.

Behzadi

B1. What is the protein corona and why does it matter?
It's the protein layer that adsorbs in biological fluids. It sets the particle's "biological identity": what cells recognise, uptake route and clearance.
B2. Which uptake route threatens your microrobots, and what's a counter?
Phagocytosis by macrophages (optimum ~1–2 µm). The counter is a PEG or other stealth coating, which reduces opsonisation.

2-minute pitch (say it out loud)

Microrobots could carry drugs to exactly the right spot in the body, but releasing the drug on command is still hard. This lab has shown that you can trap and steer tiny flower-shaped particles with focused ultrasound, even in fast blood flow, because their sharp nanosheet tips stir up local currents that make the particles clump at the focus. The same ultrasound array can image them in 3D with optoacoustics, where a laser pulse makes absorbing particles emit sound.

Separately, a Stanford group made liposomes, which are fat bubbles about 160 nanometres across, that release their drug under gentle pulsed ultrasound. The trick was adding a bit of sugar inside, which makes the core acoustically different and pulls water in once the membrane wobbles. That needs no gas and no heating.

My project combines the two. I'll make these sound-sensitive liposomes, attach them to the microrobots, trap and steer the robots with one ultrasound setting, and then release the drug with another. The key questions are whether the coating still lets the robots trap, and whether we can keep the drug in during steering and get it out on demand.

Cheat sheet

Equations

Each symbol is explained where it first appears. Typical values are in brackets.

Drag
$F_D=6\pi\eta a u$
$F_D$: Stokes drag force on a sphere [~nN]. $\eta$: dynamic viscosity of the fluid [water 1 mPa·s]. $a$: particle radius [1–2 µm]. $u$: fluid velocity relative to the particle [mm/s]. Kim writes $(\langle v\rangle-u)$, where $\langle v\rangle$ is the time-averaged streaming velocity and $u$ the particle velocity.
Radiation force
$F_R=4\pi\Phi a^3 kE_{ac}\sin 2ky$, with $E_{ac}=p_a^2/4\rho c^2$
$F_R$: acoustic radiation force [pN for one particle]. $\Phi$: acoustic contrast factor (next line). $k=2\pi/\lambda$: wavenumber, which Kim calls $q$ [8500 m⁻¹ at 2 MHz]. $\lambda$: acoustic wavelength. $E_{ac}$: acoustic energy density [~20 J/m³]. $y$: position along the standing wave. $p_a$: pressure amplitude [0.42 MPa]. $\rho$: density of the medium [1000 kg/m³]. $c$: speed of sound [1480 m/s].
Contrast factor
$\Phi=\tfrac13\big(\tfrac{5\tilde\rho-2}{2\tilde\rho+1}-\tilde\kappa\big)$, where $\tilde\rho=\rho_p/\rho_m$ and $\tilde\kappa=\kappa_p/\kappa_m$
$\rho_p,\rho_m$: density of particle and medium. $\kappa_p,\kappa_m$: compressibility of particle and medium. $\Phi>0$: the particle goes to pressure nodes (solids, ~0.2). $\Phi\lt 0$: it goes to antinodes (bubbles).
Gor'kov
$F_R=-\nabla U$, and particles go to the minima of $U$
$U$: Gor'kov potential, the effective acoustic potential energy of a small particle [J]. $\nabla$: spatial gradient. Full form: $U=\tfrac{4\pi a^3}{3}\big[\tfrac{f_1}{2\rho c^2}\langle p^2\rangle-\tfrac34 f_2\rho\langle v^2\rangle\big]$. Here $f_1=1-\kappa_p/\kappa_m$ (compressibility contrast), $f_2=\tfrac{2(\rho_p-\rho_m)}{2\rho_p+\rho_m}$ (density contrast), and $\langle p^2\rangle,\langle v^2\rangle$ are the time-averaged squared acoustic pressure and particle velocity of the fluid.
Trap regime
$2\pi r/\lambda\ll1$ means streaming drag dominates
$r$: particle radius (same as $a$) [~0.015 for Kim]. Kim calls this ratio $\kappa$; it is not the compressibility.
Boundary layer
$\delta=\sqrt{2\nu/\omega}$ ≈ 0.4 µm at 2 MHz
$\delta$: viscous boundary-layer thickness. $\nu=\eta/\rho$: kinematic viscosity [water 10⁻⁶ m²/s]. $\omega=2\pi f$: angular frequency. $f$: ultrasound frequency [Hz].
Impedance
$Z=\rho c$, $K=\rho c^2=1/\kappa$, intensity reflection $R=\big(\tfrac{Z_2-Z_1}{Z_2+Z_1}\big)^2$
$Z$: acoustic impedance [plasma 1.56 MPa·s/m]. $K$: bulk modulus [~2.4 GPa]. $\kappa$: compressibility, $1/K$. $R$: fraction of intensity reflected at a boundary between media 1 and 2. $Z_1,Z_2$: impedances on either side.
MI
$\mathrm{MI}=\mathrm{PNP[MPa]}/\sqrt{f[\mathrm{MHz}]}$ ≤ 1.9
MI: mechanical index, a dimensionless measure of cavitation risk. PNP: peak negative (rarefactional) pressure in MPa. $f$ in MHz. 1.9 is the FDA diagnostic limit.
Pulsing
duty cycle = $\tau_{\text{on}}\cdot\mathrm{PRF}$
$\tau_{\text{on}}$: burst length [50 ms in Purohit]. PRF: pulse repetition frequency [5 Hz]. Duty cycle: fraction of time sound is on [25%].
OA
$p_0=\Gamma\mu_a F$, $\Gamma=\beta c^2/C_p$, stress confinement $\tau_p\ll d/c$
$p_0$: initial optoacoustic pressure [~10 kPa]. $\Gamma$: Grüneisen parameter, dimensionless [0.1–0.2]. $\beta$: thermal expansion coefficient [1/K]. $C_p$: specific heat capacity [J/(kg·K)]. $\mu_a$: optical absorption coefficient [blood ~4 cm⁻¹ at 800 nm]. $F$: laser fluence, energy per area [≤ ~30 mJ/cm²]. $\tau_p$: laser pulse duration [~10 ns]. $d$: absorber size.
Unmixing
$\mu_a(\lambda)=\sum_i\varepsilon_i(\lambda)C_i$
$\lambda$ here is the optical wavelength [nm], not the acoustic one. $\varepsilon_i$: molar absorption spectrum of absorber $i$ (HbO, HbR, ICG). $C_i$: its concentration, which is the unknown.
Osmotic
$\Pi=\Delta c\,RT$ (0.36 osmol/L ≈ 0.9 MPa)
$\Pi$: osmotic pressure. $\Delta c$: osmolarity difference across the membrane [670 − 315 mOsm]. $R$: gas constant, 8.314 J/(mol·K), not the reflection coefficient. $T$: absolute temperature [310 K].
Release
% uncaging = free / (free + liposomal) × 100%
Free: drug measured outside the liposomes after the column. Liposomal: drug still inside.
