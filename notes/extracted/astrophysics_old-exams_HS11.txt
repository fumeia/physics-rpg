                           Astrophysics Exam


                                 31 January 2012


      I took my astrophysics exam in january 2012. My examiner was Prof. Meyer.
  He structures the exam into four short format (max. 2 points each) and two long
  format (max. 6 points each) questions. The questions are drawn from a list of
  key concepts which can be found at http://www.astro.ethz.ch/education/
  courses/Astrophysics_1. At this homepage you will also find an exact de-
  scription of the course of the exam.
      At first Prof. Meyer will describe the course of the exam, even though you
  will be probably aware of that by then. Then he will ask you what you found
  particularly surprising when you learned about astrophysics, but your answer
  does not affect your grade. He just wants to know what the students find
  interesting. Then he proceeds asking two long format and four short format
  questions. The exam takes place in his office and the atmosphere is good. If I
  would do the exam again I would not learn any derivations. I would concentrate
  on his slides plus the in-class activities, because they contain all the information
  he wants to hear in the exam! My grade was a 5.5.

Meyer: What did you find particularly surprising when studying astrophysics?
   Me: That neutron stars are so dense. I calculated that a rubber sized piece of
       a neutron star weighs as much as all humans together. Or a piece sized
       the Cheops-Pyramid weighs as much as earth.
Meyer: That is indeed very interesting! These unimaginable scales appear all the

       good at drawing with a trackpad...)

Professor Meyer is accompanied by one of the PhD student who has conducted an exercise
session during semester. After shaking hands Prof. Meyer explains the exam procedure, the
structure of 4 short questions (two minutes) and 2 long questions (six minutes), overall giving
a total of 20 points. He will start with two short questions, followed by one long and another
short question, finally ending with one long and another short question again.


Before starting, Prof. Meyer asks about what surprised me in this course and what I was
especially happy or sad about. It is a question to ease into the exam, to calm the student
down, he says. There is no wrong answer.

First Question: How can we find out about the effective temperature of a star? As I do
not understand the question correctly, Prof. Meyer tries to give me some hints and tries to
guide me in the right direction. Basically, he wants to hear Stefan-Boltzmann law, which, if we
know the radius of a star (eclipsing binary!), gives the emitted energy vs. effective temperature.

Second Question: There are two reasons for the Coulomb barrier to be penetrated although
the core temperature of a star is lower than the predicted one. I start to talk about the tunnel
effect, and that the core temperature is about 107 K but should be 1010 K. Tunneling is the
reason why this potential barrier can be overcome since the quantum mechanical probability
declines exponentially within the barrier but is not zero at the end. Prof. Meyer gives me a
hint for the second reason - Gamow peak. I write the integral formula for the nuclear reaction
rate and explain, that the product of the probability of penetrating the Coulomb barrier and
the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution is very narrow having a maximum which is called the
Gamow peak.

Third Question: Give the structure of a multiphase interstellar medium. I draw the onion-
shaped model of such a cloud with the cold neutral medium, CNM, at the core surrounded
by the warm neutral medium, WNM, and so forth. Prof. Meyer interrupts and wants to
know about the interaction between these phases. They are in pressure equilibrium given by
ideal gas law, P = nkB T . Hence, the dense core is cold and the hot outer layers have less
density. Is the cloud in pressure equilibrium with the environment? I wince, do not know the
answer. What about bulk motions with respect to Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution? I say,
they are of same order since I only can remember Doppler broadening (which has absolutely
nothing to do with this!). Prof. Meyer tries to help, we once had an in-class activity about
this! Still, memory does not come back. However, they are in overpressure, and bulk motions

Fifth Question: Write down the equation of hydrostatic equilibrium. I write it down as we
discussed it considering stellar structure. Derive the formula for the exponential atmosphere
using the ideal gas law! This I do using the ideal gas law to express density in terms of pressure

anyway. Finally, introducing the surface gravity, and using the fact that R  x given by Prof.

the density has declined by a factor 1/e. Hence, I set the exponent equal to −1 and get the
answer, H = kB T /mg. Take a larger planetary mass, what happens to the scale height? It
gets smaller since g ∼ M .

Sixth Question: There are two major properties of the cosmic microwave background, CMB. On
large scale it is homogeneous, indicating that at an early epoch the Universe must have been
in causal contact and equilibrium. Only at about one part in 105 there are inhomogeneities
- so-called anisotropies - that are the seeds for structure formation. What else do we know
about the CMB? It obeys Planck’s law - blackbody radiation at temperature T = 2.725 K.


In conclusion, Prof. Meyer is friendly and tries to calm the student. He tries to wait as long
as possible to give the student enough time to think it through again. However, there is also a
strict time pattern needed to be obeyed and Prof. Meyer pushes the student through the exam.
