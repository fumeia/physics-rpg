Exercise 1: Low-dimensional electron systems
Electronic structures with restricted dimensions can be produced at interfaces between
different materials. Two-dimensional electron systems occur, for example, at the inter-
face between silicon and silicon dioxide in so-called MOSFETs (MOSFET: Metal-Oxide-
Semiconductor Field Effect Transistor), which play a major role in today’s semiconductor
electronics. Starting from such two-dimensional electron systems, one-dimensional and
zero-dimensional structures can also be realized by lateral confinement of the electron mo-
tion using lithographic methods (so-called quantum wires, or quantum dots or ‘artificial
atoms’).

One of the most famous recently discovered materials is graphene. It consists of carbon
atoms arranged in a 2D honeycomb structure. Unlike in metals, electrons in graphene
cannot be treated as ‘free’. Instead, close to the Fermi level, the dispersion relation can
be approximated by a linear relation: ϵ(k) = ±c|k|. Note that the ± here means that
there are two energy levels at a specified k. The Fermi level is set at ϵF = 0.

  a) Make a sketch of the dispersion relation. Include both positive and negative energies.
     What other well-known particles have a linear dispersion relation?

  b) Using the dispersion relation and assuming periodic boundary conditions, calculate
     the density of states g(ϵ) of graphene. Do not forget spin degeneracy, and take into
     account that graphene has an additional two-fold ‘valley degeneracy’ (hence there
     is a total of a fourfold degeneracy instead of two). Your result should be linear with
     |ϵ|.
